Load factor at the bottom and at the top

Along a curved path of radius R at true airspeed V, the centripetal acceleration is V²/R, or V²/(gR) in g units. In a pull-up that starts from level flight, lift must also support the weight, so the load factor — lift over weight — at the bottom is

nbottom = 1 + V² / (g R)

A 100 kt pull-up with a 1,000 ft radius is about 1.89 g at the bottom. A push-over from level flight has the opposite sign, n = 1 − V²/(gR). At the top of a loop the airplane is inverted and weight helps the turn:

ntop = V² / (g R) − 1

That top load factor is only physical when V²/(gR) is greater than 1; otherwise the speed and radius cannot sustain an inverted top. This page reports all three numbers from the same V and R, and will invert any two of {V, R, nbottom} for the third: R = V² / (g(n − 1)) and V = √[g R (n − 1)].

Accelerated stall and a level-turn contrast

Stall speed rises with the square root of load factor, Vs,n = Vs √n, the same rule as the stall-speed vs weight and bank calculator. A 60 kt wings-level stall becomes 90 kt at 2.25 g. That is independent of whether the load factor comes from a pull-up or from a level banked turn (where n = 1 / cos φ). For radius and rate in a level turn, use the turn-radius calculator. For the airspeed that protects the limit load factor in turbulence, see maneuvering speed vs weight.

Sources

Frequently asked questions

What is the load factor at the bottom of a 100-knot, 1,000-foot pull-up?

About 1.89 g. n = 1 + V²/(gR), with V converted to feet per second and g = 32.174 ft/s².

When is the top of a loop possible?

When V²/(gR) is greater than 1, so n_top = V²/(gR) − 1 is positive. At 100 kt and 1,000 ft, V²/(gR) is only about 0.89, so that combination cannot sustain an inverted top.

Is this the same as load factor in a level turn?

No. A level coordinated turn has n = 1 / cos φ, with the load factor in the vertical used to provide the centripetal force in the horizontal plane. A pull-up is a vertical-plane maneuver: n = 1 + V²/(gR) at the bottom. Both raise stall speed by √n.
