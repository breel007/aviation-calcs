Planetary Transfer Calculator

A Hohmann transfer between two circular, coplanar heliocentric orbits has a time of flight of half the transfer-ellipse period and two impulsive Δv’s, one at each circular orbit. Enter a departure and an arrival planet — Earth and Mars are the default — and this page returns that time of flight, both heliocentric Δv’s, and the synodic wait until the next similar window. It is not a patched-conic departure from low Earth orbit, and it is not the single-body Hohmann calculator already on this site.

Radii default to mean semi-major axes (Earth 1 AU, Mars 1.523679 AU). The solar gravitational parameter is 1.32712440018×10²⁰ m³/s². You may also type two radii in AU.

Half an ellipse around the Sun, plus two impulsive Δv’s

The transfer semi-major axis is a_t = (r1 + r2) / 2, and the time of flight is the half-period π √(a_t³ / μ_sun). Vis-viva on that ellipse, compared with each circular speed √(μ / r), gives the two heliocentric impulses. For Earth to Mars the flight is about 259 days, with roughly 2.94 km/s at Earth’s orbit and 2.65 km/s at Mars’s, totaling about 5.59 km/s — and the next geometrically similar window is one synodic period later, about 780 days. Those Δv figures are changes of heliocentric velocity. Leaving low Earth orbit, or capturing into a Mars orbit, still has to deal with each planet’s gravity well (a patched conic or an Oberth burn this page does not compute).

The single-body Hohmann transfer calculator is the same geometry around one primary you name (Earth, or a μ you type). The synodic period page is the wait-time identity on its own, including tabulated planetary periods that differ slightly from the Keplerian periods implied by these mean radii. For a mission Δv budget you can add atmospheric or capture legs on the delta-v budget tool.

Frequently asked questions

Why is this not the Δv I would need to leave LEO?

These impulses are heliocentric. Climbing out of low Earth orbit, or braking at Mars, requires additional Δv that depends on the parking and capture orbits. A patched-conic or an Oberth burn is a different (and usually larger) number.

How is this different from the Hohmann transfer calculator?

That page is a coplanar transfer around a single body (Earth by default, or a μ you type). This one is a heliocentric planet-to-planet leg, with solar μ, AU radii, and a synodic wait.

Are planetary orbits really circular and coplanar?

No. Mars has a noticeable eccentricity, and a real trajectory is optimized around a launch window, not a pure Hohmann. This is the textbook first estimate.
