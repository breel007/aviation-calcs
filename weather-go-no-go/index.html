Route Weather Go / No-Go

See how often a week of the year and time of day historically met your personal minimums along a route, using the last 5 years of archived hourly METARs. The tool pulls observations for the departure, destination, and ASOS/AWOS stations in a corridor along the path — a great-circle, or a bent route if you add waypoints — then reports go / no-go likelihoods. A single airport is enough if you leave the destination blank. This is climatology, not a forecast.

Route

U.S. ASOS/AWOS identifiers work as ICAO or 3-letter ids (KSBA or SBA; PHNL or HNL). Optional waypoints — airports, navaids, or five-letter fixes, separated by spaces or commas — bend the corridor along each leg (for example PRB SNS, or the full KSBA PRB KSJC). A departure alone is enough if you leave the destination and route blank. You can still add or remove stations below after the corridor is built.

Week and time of day

History is the last 5 years of that week of the year. The end hour is exclusive: 07 to 10 means 07:00–09:59 local. Start and end equal means all day. An overnight window such as 22 to 06 is allowed. Times are applied in the zone you pick (defaulted from the departure airport), not each station’s own local clock, so a route that crosses a time zone stays on one timeline.

Corridor

Stations are chosen from a bundled U.S. ASOS/AWOS list, kept inside the corridor of the route (the great-circle, or each leg if you entered waypoints), and spaced along the whole path. Departure and destination are always included unless you remove them. The cap applies to automatic picks; stations you add yourself are kept even if the list grows past the cap.

    Climatology is not a forecast

    This calculator answers a planning question: in this week of the year, at this time of day, how often have the airports along this route been at or above my personal minimums? It does that by counting archived routine METARs. A 70% historical go rate for mid-June mornings is a useful prior — coastal marine layers, afternoon thunderstorms, and winter icing seasons are real patterns — but it is not a prediction for next Tuesday. Obtain an official briefing and read the METARs, TAFs, AIRMETs, SIGMETs, and convective outlooks that actually apply to the flight.

    How stations along the route are chosen

    Given a departure and destination, the page looks up each site in a compact U.S. ASOS/AWOS list (identifiers, coordinates, and time zones from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet state networks). An optional route field accepts intermediate waypoints — airports (ICAO or FAA LID), navaids, or five-letter fixes, separated by spaces or commas — such as PRB SNS or the full KSBA PRB KSJC. Stations are then taken from a corridor around that path: any station within the corridor half-width (25 NM by default) of any leg, with along-track position measured from the departure along the whole polyline. If you leave the waypoint field blank, the path is the single great-circle between the two airports. A destination is also optional: a departure alone, or the same identifier in both boxes, scores that one airport. Those candidates are thinned to a capped, evenly spaced set — departure and destination first, then the largest remaining gaps along the route — so a 200 NM hop does not fire a request for every AWOS on the coast. You can remove any of them, add an alternate or a site the auto-picker skipped, and widen or narrow the corridor. A route go requires every selected station to meet the minimums in the same hour, so the table also shows each station alone — the lowest one is the historically weakest segment. Auto-picked sites with a short or patchy archive (for example a station that only came online in the last year or two) are still listed, but they are left out of that combined route score so they do not erase earlier years of data.

    How a go hour and a go day are counted

    For the chosen month and day, the tool takes a window of ±3 days (a seven-day week; you can shorten or lengthen it) in each of the last 5 years. Times are converted from the archive’s UTC stamps into one time zone — the departure zone unless you change it — and only observations in your local hour window are scored. The end hour is exclusive (07–10 is 07:00–09:59). A single observation is a go when:

    • The ceiling is at least your minimum, or there is no ceiling (clear, few, or scattered only). Ceiling is the lowest BKN, OVC, or VV layer, as in AIM Chapter 7. Heights come from the archive in feet AGL.
    • Visibility is at least your minimum, in statute miles.
    • If you excluded rain, snow, freezing precipitation, or thunderstorms/severe weather, none of those present-weather codes appear. Severe here follows the list on the form: thunderstorms (including vicinity), heavy rain (+RA), hail, funnel clouds, and squalls — from AIM 7-1 contractions.
    • Optional wind and gust caps, if you set them, are not exceeded. A missing gust is not treated as a gust-limit failure.

    Observations that are missing a field you are actually checking (no sky condition, or no visibility) are dropped from the denominator rather than scored as a no-go. A route-go hour is one in which every selected station has an evaluable observation and all of them are go. The headline figure is the share of historical days in the window that had at least one route-go hour — “a go window existed that morning.” The page also reports the stricter share of hours that were simultaneously go, and the share of days on which every hour in the window was a go (“a solid morning”).

    The archive, sampling, and request limits

    Data are fetched in your browser from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet ASOS/AWOS download service (mesonet.agron.iastate.edu), which allows cross-origin requests. The URL asks only for sky cover and height, visibility, weather codes, and wind, for the week window in each year (padded by one UTC day so local midnights are not clipped), and only for routine METARs (report_type=3). Special observations cluster in deteriorating weather and would bias the climatology toward no-go if they were included. Several stations are combined in one request per year. The Iowa Environmental Mesonet allows about one request every two seconds from a given IP, so the page spaces year-windows out and shows results as each year arrives. Responses are cached in localStorage so a repeat check with the same stations and dates is instant. Press “Check historical go / no-go” to load the archive. After the data is in, changing minimums or the time-of-day window updates the results immediately without another download. Please do not script heavy downloads against IEM from this idea — the page is intentionally light. IEM station identifiers for the lower 48 are usually the three-letter id (KSBA is SBA in the archive); Alaska, Hawaii, and many non-CONUS sites use the four-letter ICAO.

    VFR minimums and flight category

    The VFR preset (3,000 ft and 5 SM), MVFR-or-better preset (1,000 and 3), and IFR personal preset (500 and 1, plus freezing precipitation and severe weather excluded) follow the familiar AIM flight-category breakpoints as a starting point for personal minimums. Comparisons are inclusive. They are not a restatement of 14 CFR 91.155, whose basic VFR visibility and cloud-clearance values depend on airspace class and altitude and are sometimes lower than “flight category VFR,” sometimes not. Use the legal rule that applies to the airspace, then apply your own tighter minimums here if you wish.

    Frequently asked questions

    What does this route weather go / no-go calculator tell me?

    How often, in the same week of the year and time of day, archived hourly METARs at the selected airports along your route met the personal minimums you entered. A route go means every selected station was a go in the same hour. Optional waypoints bend the corridor; a single airport is enough if you leave the destination and route blank.

    Is this a weather forecast?

    No. It is climatology and says nothing reliable about the actual day. Get an official briefing.

    How is a route go decided?

    All selected stations must have an evaluable routine METAR that meets every limit in that local hour. The headline day percentage is the share of historical sessions with at least one such hour. Per-station columns show which airport is the usual weak point.

    Where does the historical weather come from?

    The Iowa Environmental Mesonet ASOS/AWOS archive, requested from your browser, routine hourly METARs only, with light, cached 5-year week-window downloads.

    How is ceiling defined, and how do the VFR presets relate to 14 CFR 91.155?

    Ceiling is the lowest BKN, OVC, or VV layer (AIM). The presets are AIM-style flight-category starting points for personal minimums, not a substitute for 91.155.

    Can I enter a route with waypoints, not just a straight line?

    Yes. Type intermediate waypoints in the route field — airport identifiers, navaids, or five-letter fixes — separated by spaces or commas, for example PRB SNS or the full KSBA PRB KSJC. Stations are picked in a 25 NM corridor of any leg and spaced along the whole path. Leave the field blank and the path is the great-circle between departure and destination.

    Does the weather lookup work with only one location?

    Yes. Enter a departure and leave the destination and route blank, or put the same identifier in both airport boxes. The page then scores that one airport’s archived METARs for the chosen week and time of day.
