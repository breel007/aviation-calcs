How to calculate glide distance

Best-glide ratio is distance over altitude in the same units — 9:1 means 9 feet forward for every foot of altitude you give up. Still-air range in nautical miles is

distance (NM) = (altitude AGL in feet / 6,076.12) × glide ratio

The corresponding descent angle is arctan(1 / ratio). A 9:1 airplane descends at about 6.3°.

Adding wind

The time to come down does not care about the wind: it is altitude divided by the still-air sink rate, and sink rate is best-glide TAS divided by the ratio. Ground distance is then groundspeed times that time. A headwind shortens the range; a tailwind stretches it. In a uniform wind the reachable area is a circle of still-air radius, slid downwind by wind speed times time — you can go farther with the wind at your back, and the worst case is straight into it.

Worked example

6,000 ft AGL, 9:1, 70 kt best-glide TAS, 10 kt headwind:

  • Still-air distance ≈ 8.89 NM (47,000+ ft, about 10.2 statute miles). Time to the ground is a little over 7 minutes.
  • Into the 10 kt headwind the range shrinks to about 7.6 NM; downwind it grows to about 10.2 NM.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate glide distance from altitude?

Multiply AGL by the glide ratio and convert feet to nautical miles (divide by 6,076). Then correct for wind if you have a best-glide speed.

How does wind affect best-glide range?

Time to descend stays the same; ground distance is groundspeed times that time. The reachable area is the still-air circle shifted downwind.

What is a typical best-glide ratio for a light airplane?

Many trainers are around 8:1 to 9:1. Use the POH figure for the airplane you actually fly.

Can I use this in a real engine-out?

Use it for planning and a first estimate. In the airplane, fly the POH speed, pick a field, and fly the airplane. This page is not a substitute for the book or for judgment.
