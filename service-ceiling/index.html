Service Ceiling Calculator

A simple and widely used model is that rate of climb falls in a straight line as density altitude rises, reaching zero at the absolute ceiling. The service ceiling is the altitude where climb has fallen to a chosen residual — 100 feet per minute for most light airplanes. Enter a sea-level climb and an absolute ceiling, or two climb rates at two density altitudes, and this page fits that line and reports the service ceiling and the climb rate at any height you ask for.

The usual light-airplane service residual is 100 ft/min; some manuals use 50 or 500. Climb must decrease as altitude increases for a two-point fit to make sense.

A straight line from sea-level climb to the absolute ceiling

The linear model is ROC(h) = ROC0 (1 − h/habs). Set ROC equal to the service residual ROCsvc (100 ft/min unless you change it) and the service ceiling is hsvc = habs (1 − ROCsvc/ROC0). A sea-level 1,200 ft/min and a 12,000-foot absolute ceiling therefore have an 11,000-foot service ceiling, and 600 ft/min left at 6,000 feet. If you have climb rates at two density altitudes instead, the page fits the same straight line.

Real climb performance is not exactly linear, and it depends on weight, configuration, and temperature in ways a two-point fit cannot see. A POH climb chart always wins. For the density altitude you are actually departing from, use the density-altitude calculator; for a gradient in feet per nautical mile, use climb gradient.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the default service residual 100 ft/min?

That is the usual light-airplane definition of service ceiling. Some jet or special-performance manuals use a different residual; type the number your book uses.

Can I use indicated altitude instead of density altitude?

The model is in density altitude. Using indicated or pressure altitude on a non-standard day will misplace the ceiling.

What if climb increases with altitude in my two points?

The page will not fit that pair. A service-ceiling line has to slope down.
