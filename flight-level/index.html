Flight Level Calculator

A flight level is just pressure altitude written in hundreds of feet with the altimeter set to 29.92 inHg (1013.25 hPa). This page converts between feet of pressure altitude and an FL number, then tells you whether a climbing or descending aircraft at that altitude would still be on QNH or already on the standard setting, given the transition altitude and transition level you enter. The United States defaults (18,000 ft and FL180) are filled in; elsewhere you type the published figures.

Enter either a pressure altitude in feet or a flight level (180 and 18000 are both accepted for FL180). Transition altitude is in feet; transition level may be entered as 70 or 7000.

FL = pressure altitude ÷ 100

With 29.92 inHg (1013.25 hPa) in the Kollsman window, the altimeter reads pressure altitude. A flight level is that reading divided by 100, usually written with three digits: 18,000 ft pressure altitude is FL180. Below the published transition altitude you fly QNH altitudes; at or above the published transition level you fly flight levels. In the United States both numbers are 18,000 ft / FL180. In other airspaces they can differ, leaving a transition layer in between so that a climbing aircraft changes to 29.92 at the TA and a descending aircraft changes back to QNH at the TL.

The optional QNH box applies the same 1,000 ft per inHg rule of thumb used on the pressure-altitude calculator: PA ≈ QNH altitude + 1,000 × (29.92 − QNH in inHg). It is a quick offset, not a hydrostatic integration — for a temperature-aware true altitude see the true-altitude page.

What this does not assign

RVSM, hemispheric rules, and the actual flight level you are cleared to are ATC products. A low regional QNH can raise the lowest usable flight level above the published TL; this page does not compute that. Always set the altimeter as required by 14 CFR 91.121 or the equivalent national rule, and use current ATIS or ATC settings rather than a remembered 29.92 below the transition.

Frequently asked questions

Is FL180 always 18,000 feet on the altimeter?

It is 18,000 feet of pressure altitude — the reading you get with 29.92 set. If you still have a local QNH in the window, the same flight level is a different indicated number.

Why can the transition altitude and transition level differ?

Some states publish a TA in feet and a TL as a flight level high enough that even on a low QNH there is a buffer (the transition layer) between QNH altitudes and flight levels. The United States uses the same 18,000 ft / FL180 for both.

Does this change my RVSM or cruising-level rules?

No. It only converts the numbers and shows which altimeter setting a published TA/TL would call for. Cruising levels and RVSM come from the airspace and the clearance.
