Sun-Synchronous Orbit Calculator

A sun-synchronous orbit keeps a nearly constant local time at the ascending node because Earth’s oblateness precesses the line of nodes at the same rate the mean Sun appears to move along the equator — about 0.985647 degrees per day. Enter an altitude (and an optional eccentricity) and this page solves the J2 nodal-precession formula for that inclination. It does not recompute a geostationary radius; that lives on the orbital-velocity page.

Altitude is above the WGS-84 equator (R_eq = 6,378.137 km); the semi-major axis is R_eq plus that altitude. Leave eccentricity at 0 for a circular orbit. J2 defaults to 1.08263×10⁻³, and the target precession defaults to 360° per tropical year.

Inclination from the J2 nodal rate

The first-order nodal precession of an Earth orbit is Ω̇ = −(3/2) n J2 (R_eq / a)² cos i / (1−e²)², with n = √(μ / a³). Setting Ω̇ to the mean apparent solar rate, 360° / 365.24219 days ≈ 0.985647 °/day, and solving for i gives a retrograde inclination (cos i is negative). At 800 km, circular, with J2 = 1.08263×10⁻³ and R_eq = 6,378.137 km, that inclination is about 98.60°, and the period is about 101 minutes. Higher altitudes need a slightly shallower (closer to 90°) inclination because n and (R/a)² both fall off.

Higher-order gravity, drag, and lunisolar perturbations all move a real SSO off this first-order i. Use an official ephemeris or a mission-design tool for a frozen or repeating ground-track orbit. Geostationary radius, circular speed, and vis-viva are on the orbital velocity and period page; a nadir footprint is on satellite FOV.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the inclination more than 90°?

J2 regresses the nodes of a prograde orbit (i < 90°). A sun-synchronous orbit needs the nodes to advance with the mean Sun, so the orbit is slightly retrograde.

Can I use this for Mars or the Moon?

Not with the default constants. The formula is general, but this page is wired to Earth’s μ, J2, and equatorial radius.

Is this the same as a geostationary orbit?

No. A geostationary orbit is equatorial and has a one-day period. Sun-synchronous orbits are usually low, polar, and retrograde. Geostationary radius is on the orbital-velocity page.
